For about two weeks each year, around the start of June, the forest at Elkmont in the Great Smoky Mountains does something that sounds invented. As the last light goes, thousands of male fireflies of a single species begin to flash. Each gives a quick burst of flashes, then goes dark for several seconds. At first the bursts come from everywhere at once. As more fireflies join in, the bursts line up, until the whole hillside flashes together, goes black together, and flashes again.

The species is Photinus carolinus. Of roughly two thousand kinds of firefly, only a handful flash in unison. The other famous ones are the Pteroptyx fireflies of Southeast Asia, which gather in riverside trees and flash together for hours.

Reports like this were once dismissed. In 1917 a letter in the journal Science declared that such behaviour would be “contrary to all natural laws”, and suggested that the real cause was the twitching of the observer’s own eyelids. The fireflies were real. The interesting part is the puzzle underneath: no firefly can see the whole forest, and none of them is in charge. So who is keeping time?

Part one

The rhythm of one firefly

Start with one. Forget wings, chemistry and courtship. To a mathematician a firefly is a clock: something that runs through the same cycle again and again, and flashes once per cycle. All we need to know is where in its cycle it is.

So draw the cycle as a circle and the firefly as a dot travelling round it. Where the dot is on the circle is called the firefly’s phase. Each time the dot passes the mark on the right, the firefly flashes.

Take the dot round by hand in Figure 1. You are now doing the firefly’s job, and the curve beside the circle is keeping a record of how high the dot is. Going round and round a circle, and going up and down in a wave, turn out to be the same motion seen from two sides. The wave has a name you have met before: sine.

How fast the dot travels is the firefly’s natural frequency. No two fireflies are quite alike: some run a little fast, some a little slow. Remember that. It is the villain of this story.

A real flash is chemistry. In the firefly’s lantern an enzyme called luciferase helps oxygen react with a molecule called luciferin, and the reaction gives off light. We keep only the timing.

Part two

When fireflies interact

Now give the clocks eyes. Put two fireflies on a branch, one that runs slightly fast and one slightly slow, and suppose each has a single habit. When it sees the other flashing ahead of it, it hurries a little. When it sees the other behind, it holds back. How strongly it reacts is the coupling, the dial you turned on the cover.

Two things are now pulling against each other. The difference in their natural speeds drags the pair apart. The coupling draws them together. Figure 2 lets you referee.

Try this. Leave the difference at 0.60 and bring the coupling up slowly from zero. Below 0.60 the fast firefly keeps lapping the slow one. Above 0.60 they lock, and flash at the same rate for as long as you care to watch. That is the whole law for a pair: they lock when the coupling is at least as large as the difference in their speeds.

Two fireflies fall into step exactly when their willingness to listen outweighs their difference in temperament.

Look closely at a locked pair, though. They do not flash at the same instant. The naturally faster one stays a little ahead. Synchrony here means agreeing on a tempo, with each player keeping a small lead or lag of its own.

With a forest instead of a pair, the habit stays the same; there are just more neighbours. Each firefly looks at the others it can see, works out whether it is on average ahead of them or behind, and adjusts. Nothing in that rule mentions the forest as a whole.

Part three

Measuring collective order

It is easy to watch that sky and feel that it has synchronised. A feeling is not a measurement, and a pretty animation can fool the person who made it as easily as the person watching. We need a number.

Here is Kuramoto’s trick. Turn each firefly’s phase into an arrow of length one, pointing at wherever its dot sits on the circle. Now average the arrows: lay them head to tail, see how far you have travelled, and divide by the number of arrows. If the fireflies agree, the arrows march off in a line and the average is long, close to 1. If they disagree, the arrows wander back over themselves and the average is short.

The length of that average arrow is called , the order parameter. It is the number on the cover and the percentage in the laboratory.

One warning, which Figure 4 lets you check. Push the spread to its maximum, so the arrows are completely random, and shuffle a few times. The answer is never zero. Twelve random arrows give an of about 0.26 on average, by chance alone; 150 fireflies give about 0.07. A small is evidence of nothing. Part of measuring well is knowing what “nothing” looks like.

Why it works: agreement feeds itself

Now the cover can be explained. Suppose a few fireflies happen to agree. Their arrows add up, so the average arrow gets a little longer. Every firefly is pulled towards that average, and the pull is proportional to its length. So a little agreement makes a pull, the pull makes more agreement, and more agreement makes a stronger pull.

Order feeds itself. That is why the curve on the cover does not climb steadily: it starts slowly and then accelerates. It is also why synchrony can fail completely. The loop has to get started. If natural speeds are too varied for that first weak pull to hold on to anyone, nothing ever builds.

When it fails: the tipping point

So will any amount of listening bring a forest into step, given long enough? No, and this is the surprise at the centre of the subject. Below a critical coupling a crowd stays a crowd for ever. Above it, order switches on.

That gives three different ways for a forest to fail, and you can watch each of them in the laboratory.

Too individual

The coupling is below the tipping point. Everyone can see everyone, and it makes no difference.

Too noisy

Random jostling knocks the phases apart as fast as coupling gathers them. Noise lifts the tipping point itself: watch the dotted tick on the coupling dial jump past the handle.

Too short-sighted

Each firefly sees only a small patch. Neighbourhoods agree quickly; the forest takes far longer, and may not get there.

What the model leaves out

A model is a deliberate simplification. This one keeps a single idea: rhythm plus mutual adjustment is enough to create order, and only past a threshold. It is right about that idea, and silent about a great deal of firefly biology. So it is worth being exact about what was left out.

Start with the largest omission. In our model a firefly feels its neighbours all the time, smoothly, as if it could read their clocks. A real firefly sees nothing at all until a neighbour flashes. Then it gets one sudden nudge.

In 1990 Renato Mirollo and Steven Strogatz studied clocks that talk only in pulses like that, building on a model Charles Peskin had made of the heart’s pacemaker cells. Figure 7 is their model, running. Twenty identical fireflies charge up, flash, and start again. Every flash lifts all the others a little closer to their own.

The stripes can only merge. Once a flash carries a second firefly over the top, the two flash together, feel exactly the same nudges from then on, and stay together for good. Mirollo and Strogatz proved that this goes all the way: from almost any starting arrangement, identical fireflies that can all see each other end as a single rhythm.

Compare that ending with the laboratory’s. There, a synchronised swarm still shows an arc of dots on the phase circle, fast fireflies leading and slow ones trailing, and stops short of 1. Here there is no arc. The unison is exact, and it arrives in a finite time. Two models, two different roads to one beat. Real fireflies are under no obligation to take either.

The curve flattens towards the top, so is smallest there, and the same flash advances a firefly that is nearly ready by more than one that has only just begun. The bend is essential: along a straight line every firefly would be advanced equally and no gap would ever close. Using only the facts that rises and bends downwards, Mirollo and Strogatz proved that for any number of identical oscillators, all able to see each other, the starting states that fail to reach unison form a set of measure zero.

Four more things neither model includes

  • Not every firefly is a steady clock. Field recordings of Photinus carolinus published in 2023 found that a male on his own flashes in bursts at irregular intervals. The regular rhythm of the swarm appears only in a group. For that species, the “clock” of Part one is itself something the crowd creates.
  • Real fireflies can retune. Our fireflies never change their natural speed, which is why a locked pair in Figure 2 keeps a permanent gap. Some Southeast Asian species do better. Paced by a rhythm slightly different from their own, Pteroptyx malaccae shift their own tempo until the gap disappears. Bard Ermentrout’s 1991 model adds exactly that ability.
  • Seeing is complicated. Real fireflies have lines of sight blocked by leaves and take time to react, and in the Smokies it is the flying males that synchronise while females answer from the ground. We use a circle of radius R and instant reactions.
  • Our forest is small and flat. 150 fireflies on a rectangle whose edges wrap round, where a real display has thousands in three dimensions.

The same story elsewhere

Wherever many rhythms can sense each other, this mathematics has something to say. Sometimes what it says is “not so fast”.

February 1665

Two pendulum clocks

Christiaan Huygens, unwell and confined to his room, noticed that two clocks hanging from one beam always ended up swinging in exact opposition. Disturb them, and within half an hour they were back. He called it an odd kind of sympathy. The beam was carrying tiny movements between them.

About once a second

Your heartbeat

Every beat starts in the sinoatrial node, a cluster of thousands of pacemaker cells. Each cell can keep a rhythm of its own. Coupled together, they fire as one.

June 2000

The Millennium Bridge

London’s new footbridge swayed so much that it closed two days after opening. For years it was the textbook case of walkers falling into step. A 2021 study argues the reverse: people keeping their balance on a moving deck feed the sway even when out of step, and any synchrony is a result of the wobble, not its cause.

After a good concert

Applause

Scattered clapping sometimes falls into a single rhythm. Physicists who recorded theatre audiences in 2000 found the crowd does it by clapping more slowly, which narrows the spread of tempos. In this issue’s terms, they turn down the individuality dial.

50 times a second

The power grid

Every generator on Britain’s grid has to turn in step. Engineers study when a grid stays locked together, and when it can fall apart, with equations closely related to Kuramoto’s.
